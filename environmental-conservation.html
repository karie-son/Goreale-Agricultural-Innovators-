UNDERSTANDING OUR ENVIRONMENT

What Is Environmental Conservation?

Environmental conservation refers to the responsible protection, management and sustainable use of natural resources and ecosystems. It involves safeguarding forests, rangelands, wetlands, rivers, soils, wildlife, biodiversity and other components of the natural environment while ensuring that present and future generations can continue benefiting from them.

Conservation is not simply about preventing environmental destruction. It also involves restoring degraded landscapes, improving natural resource management, reducing pollution, protecting biodiversity and encouraging communities to adopt practices that maintain ecological balance.

Healthy ecosystems support agriculture, livestock production, water availability, food security, climate regulation and community livelihoods. Environmental conservation is therefore closely connected to sustainable agricultural development and the long-term wellbeing of society.

For GOREALE Agricultural Innovators, environmental conservation is an important part of promoting sustainable agriculture, agroforestry, climate resilience, responsible livestock management and community participation in natural resource protection.

CONSERVATION FOUNDATIONS

Core Principles of Environmental Conservation

Effective conservation requires a balanced approach that considers ecological health, community needs, economic activities and long-term environmental sustainability.

Protection of Nature

Safeguarding forests, wildlife, water sources, soils and natural habitats against destruction, pollution and unsustainable exploitation.

Sustainable Resource Use

Using natural resources responsibly while allowing ecosystems to recover and maintain their ability to support future generations.

Ecosystem Restoration

Rehabilitating degraded landscapes through appropriate vegetation recovery, soil protection, water management and habitat restoration.

Biodiversity Protection

Conserving the diversity of plants, animals and microorganisms that maintain ecological functions and ecosystem resilience.

Pollution Prevention

Reducing harmful waste, chemicals, emissions and other pollutants that threaten human health and natural ecosystems.

Community Participation

Involving local communities, farmers, pastoralists, youth, institutions and other stakeholders in environmental decisions and action.

KENYA'S NATURAL HERITAGE

Understanding Kenya's Ecosystems

Kenya has diverse ecological systems that support agriculture, livestock, tourism, biodiversity, water resources and millions of livelihoods. Each ecosystem requires conservation approaches suited to its environmental conditions.

Forests and Woodlands

Forests and woodlands provide habitat for wildlife, protect watersheds, store carbon, regulate local climates and support livelihoods through sustainable forest products.

Conservation activities include protecting existing forests, preventing illegal logging, restoring degraded forest areas, promoting appropriate indigenous tree species and managing forest resources sustainably.

Rangelands and Grasslands

Rangelands and grasslands support pastoralism, livestock production, wildlife habitats and important ecological processes.

Their conservation requires sustainable grazing management, protection of natural vegetation, restoration of degraded grazing areas, prevention of soil erosion and responsible management of livestock and wildlife interactions.

Wetlands and Water Ecosystems

Wetlands, rivers, lakes, springs and other water ecosystems provide water, support biodiversity, regulate water flows and contribute to livelihoods.

Conservation requires protecting water catchments, maintaining vegetation along waterways, preventing dumping and pollution, and avoiding activities that damage sensitive wetland habitats.

Coastal and Marine Ecosystems

Kenya's coastal and marine environments include mangroves, coral reefs, seagrass beds, beaches and marine habitats.

These ecosystems support fisheries, biodiversity, coastal livelihoods and natural protection against environmental pressures. Their conservation requires sustainable fishing, pollution control and habitat protection.

Mountain and Highland Ecosystems

Kenya's mountain and highland landscapes contain important forests, water catchments, agricultural areas and diverse habitats.

Conservation includes protecting catchment vegetation, controlling soil erosion, maintaining forest cover and managing agricultural land sustainably.

Dryland and Semi-Arid Ecosystems

Arid and semi-arid lands are characterized by limited and variable rainfall, high evaporation, water scarcity and vegetation adapted to dry conditions.

Their conservation depends on appropriate grazing management, indigenous vegetation protection, rainwater harvesting, soil restoration and drought-resilient land-use practices.

ENVIRONMENTAL PRESSURES

Major Environmental Challenges in Kenya

Environmental degradation can result from natural pressures, unsustainable land use, pollution, poorly managed development and changing climatic conditions. These pressures often interact and intensify one another.

Deforestation and Vegetation Loss

Uncontrolled tree cutting, land clearing, unsustainable fuelwood collection and poorly planned development can reduce natural vegetation cover.

The consequences may include habitat loss, declining biodiversity, soil exposure, reduced carbon storage and pressure on water catchments.

Soil Erosion and Land Degradation

Wind and water erosion remove fertile topsoil and reduce the productive capacity of land. Poor land management, vegetation loss and concentrated runoff can accelerate degradation.

Soil erosion may contribute to sedimentation in water bodies, reduced agricultural productivity and expansion of degraded land.

Unsustainable Grazing

Excessive grazing pressure in particular locations, especially around permanent water points, can prevent vegetation recovery and expose soil to erosion.

Appropriate grazing arrangements, mobility, resting periods and rangeland restoration can help improve ecosystem condition.

Water Scarcity and Pollution

Water scarcity can be intensified by unreliable rainfall, increasing demand, degraded catchments and inefficient water use.

Pollution from untreated waste, chemicals, poor sanitation and inappropriate disposal practices can threaten water quality and aquatic ecosystems.

Loss of Biodiversity

Habitat destruction, illegal exploitation, pollution, invasive species and climate pressures can threaten wildlife and plant populations.

Biodiversity loss can weaken ecosystem functions such as pollination, natural pest control and ecological resilience.

Waste and Plastic Pollution

Improper disposal of plastic, packaging, electronic waste, household refuse and agricultural waste can pollute land and water.

Open burning and uncontrolled dumping can also create risks for human health, livestock, wildlife and the environment.

Climate Change and Extreme Events

Changing rainfall patterns, rising temperatures, droughts and floods can affect ecosystems, agriculture, livestock and community livelihoods.

Climate adaptation and ecosystem restoration are important for strengthening resilience.

Invasive Alien Species

Some introduced species can spread aggressively and compete with native plants and animals, alter habitats or affect agricultural production.

Identification, monitoring, prevention and responsible management should follow competent local guidance.

NORTHEASTERN KENYA AND ARID LANDS

Environmental Conservation in Garissa and Northeastern Kenya

Garissa County and neighbouring parts of northeastern Kenya are predominantly characterized by arid and semi-arid conditions. Communities depend on livestock, agriculture in suitable locations, natural vegetation, groundwater and other natural resources for their livelihoods.

Environmental conservation in these landscapes must account for rainfall variability, drought, high temperatures, limited water availability, mobile pastoral livelihoods and the ecological importance of natural rangelands.

1. Protecting Natural Rangelands

Rangelands are important for livestock production, wildlife, soil protection and the maintenance of dryland biodiversity. Their management should recognize seasonal grazing patterns, available forage, water distribution and the need for vegetation recovery.

  • Develop community-supported grazing plans based on local conditions and available pasture.
  • Allow degraded grazing areas to recover through appropriately planned resting periods.
  • Avoid concentrating livestock continuously around limited water points.
  • Protect valuable native grasses, shrubs and trees.
  • Restore degraded areas using suitable locally sourced indigenous vegetation.
  • Monitor pasture condition and adjust grazing pressure during drought and recovery periods.

2. Dryland Tree Conservation

Indigenous trees and shrubs are important components of dryland ecosystems. Depending on the species and site, they can provide shade, fodder, habitat, soil protection, useful products and other ecosystem benefits.

Examples of indigenous dryland tree and shrub species that may be considered for local assessment include:

  • Vachellia tortilis (umbrella thorn).
  • Balanites aegyptiaca (desert date).
  • Salvadora persica (toothbrush tree).
  • Ziziphus spina-christi (Christ's thorn jujube).

These are examples for consideration, not a universal planting prescription. Species selection should be based on local ecological suitability, provenance, soil conditions, water availability, community needs and advice from qualified forestry personnel.

3. Soil and Water Conservation

Dryland soils are vulnerable to wind erosion, runoff and declining organic matter where vegetation cover is lost. Conservation measures should slow runoff, improve infiltration and protect the soil surface.

  • Use locally appropriate contour structures where the terrain and soil conditions support them.
  • Harvest rainwater from suitable roofs and catchment surfaces.
  • Protect water pans, wells and other community water sources from contamination.
  • Maintain ground cover and vegetation where possible.
  • Use organic matter and suitable soil improvement practices.
  • Manage runoff to reduce destructive concentration of flowing water.

4. Managing Human and Livestock Pressure

Conservation initiatives should involve pastoralists, farmers, community leaders, women and youth. Local knowledge about grazing routes, seasonal resources, drought conditions and traditional land management can improve conservation planning.

Solutions should support livelihoods while protecting the ecological resources on which those livelihoods depend.

FORESTS • TREES • AGROFORESTRY

Forest Conservation, Afforestation and Agroforestry

Trees and forests contribute to environmental stability by supporting biodiversity, protecting soils, influencing local water cycles, storing carbon and providing resources for communities.

Forest Conservation

Forest conservation involves protecting existing forest ecosystems from unsustainable exploitation, degradation, encroachment and destructive activities. It also includes supporting sustainable management and community participation in forest protection.

Afforestation and Reforestation

Afforestation involves establishing tree cover in areas that have not recently supported forest cover, while reforestation involves restoring tree cover in areas where forests have been lost or degraded.

Successful tree-growing programmes require more than distributing seedlings. They need appropriate species selection, site preparation, planting at suitable times, protection, maintenance and monitoring of survival.

Agroforestry

Agroforestry integrates trees and shrubs with crops and/or livestock within agricultural landscapes. When appropriately designed, it can support soil protection, shade, fodder, useful tree products, biodiversity and livelihood diversification.

Practical Tree-Growing Recommendations

  • Select species suited to local rainfall, soils and intended uses.
  • Prioritize indigenous species appropriate to the local ecosystem.
  • Obtain healthy planting material from reputable and suitable sources.
  • Plant when soil moisture conditions are suitable, preferably at the beginning of dependable rains.
  • Protect young trees against browsing, trampling and other damage.
  • Use water efficiently and establish realistic maintenance arrangements.
  • Record seedlings planted, survival, growth and replacement needs.
  • Avoid planting trees in unsuitable habitats or where they could damage sensitive grassland or water systems.

Important: Tree planting is not automatically equivalent to ecosystem restoration. Restoration should begin with understanding the original ecosystem, its natural vegetation and the environmental pressures affecting it. In some locations, protecting natural regeneration or restoring grassland may be more appropriate than establishing tree plantations.

LAND HEALTH AND PRODUCTIVITY

Soil Conservation and Sustainable Land Management

Soil is a vital natural resource that supports plant growth, agriculture, vegetation recovery, water infiltration and many ecosystem processes. Healthy soils are essential for food production, livestock systems and environmental sustainability.

Common Causes of Soil Degradation

  • Wind and water erosion.
  • Loss of protective vegetation cover.
  • Unsustainable cultivation practices.
  • Excessive grazing pressure.
  • Declining soil organic matter.
  • Soil compaction and inappropriate land use.
  • Pollution and unsuitable chemical use.

Practical Soil Conservation Measures

  • Maintain vegetation cover to reduce direct exposure of soil to wind and rainfall impact.
  • Apply mulching and suitable organic matter management.
  • Use contour farming and erosion-control structures where appropriate.
  • Establish vegetative barriers and windbreaks using suitable species.
  • Avoid unnecessary disturbance of vulnerable soils.
  • Manage grazing to allow vegetation recovery.
  • Use crop rotations and suitable soil fertility practices.
  • Monitor areas affected by gullies, erosion and declining productivity.

Restoring Degraded Land

Land restoration should begin by identifying the cause and extent of degradation. Depending on the site, interventions may include natural regeneration, controlled grazing, reseeding with suitable native grasses, erosion control, water harvesting, agroforestry or rehabilitation of degraded agricultural land.

WATER SECURITY

Water Conservation and Catchment Protection

Water is fundamental to human survival, agriculture, livestock production, ecosystem functioning and economic development. Protecting water resources requires responsible use, pollution prevention and the conservation of landscapes that influence water quality and availability.

Rainwater Harvesting

Rainwater harvesting involves collecting and storing rainfall for appropriate uses. Depending on local conditions, it may involve roof catchments, storage tanks, lined reservoirs, water pans and other suitable systems.

Water-Efficient Agricultural Practices

  • Use irrigation methods suited to crop requirements and available water resources.
  • Repair leaks and maintain water distribution systems.
  • Use mulching and soil management to reduce unnecessary moisture loss.
  • Schedule irrigation according to crop needs and local conditions.
  • Harvest rainwater where technically, environmentally and legally appropriate.

Protecting Water Sources

  • Prevent dumping of waste into rivers, wells, water pans and wetlands.
  • Protect suitable vegetation around water bodies.
  • Manage livestock access to water sources to reduce contamination and physical damage.
  • Maintain appropriate sanitation and wastewater management.
  • Monitor water quality where community or project resources allow.

Water harvesting structures, groundwater abstraction and activities affecting rivers or wetlands may require technical assessment, permits or authorization from relevant authorities. Consult the Water Resources Authority and applicable county and national agencies before undertaking regulated activities.

LIFE AND ECOLOGICAL BALANCE

Biodiversity Conservation and Wildlife Protection

Biodiversity refers to the variety of life, including different species, genetic diversity and ecosystems. It includes plants, animals, microorganisms and the ecological relationships that connect them.

Why Biodiversity Matters

  • Supports ecosystem stability and resilience.
  • Contributes to pollination and natural pest regulation.
  • Supports food systems and agricultural productivity.
  • Provides habitat and resources for wildlife.
  • Contributes to cultural, educational and economic values.
  • Supports ecosystem processes such as nutrient cycling and soil formation.

Practical Biodiversity Protection

  • Protect natural habitats and ecological corridors.
  • Avoid unnecessary destruction of native vegetation.
  • Reduce pollution and harmful chemical exposure.
  • Support responsible wildlife protection and reporting of suspected illegal activities.
  • Identify and manage invasive species using approved methods.
  • Promote community education about native species and ecosystem values.
  • Encourage coexistence between people, livestock and wildlife.
POLLUTION PREVENTION

Waste Management and Environmental Pollution Control

Responsible waste management protects public health, soil, water resources, wildlife and the wider environment. Poorly managed waste can contaminate land and water, create breeding sites for disease vectors and introduce harmful substances into ecosystems.

The Waste Management Hierarchy

  1. Refuse: Avoid unnecessary products and materials that create avoidable waste.
  2. Reduce: Minimize the amount of resources and materials consumed.
  3. Reuse: Use suitable materials again rather than disposing of them.
  4. Repair: Maintain equipment and products to extend their useful life.
  5. Recycle: Separate suitable recyclable materials for responsible recovery.
  6. Recover and dispose: Ensure remaining waste is managed through appropriate authorized systems.

Organic and Agricultural Waste

Appropriate organic residues may be composted or reused where safe and suitable. Crop residues, manure and other organic materials can contribute to soil improvement when properly managed.

However, diseased plant material, contaminated waste and hazardous agricultural chemicals require appropriate handling and disposal. They should not automatically be added to compost.

Plastic and Packaging Waste

  • Reduce unnecessary single-use materials.
  • Separate recyclable materials where collection systems exist.
  • Avoid dumping plastics in open land, drainage channels and water bodies.
  • Use authorized waste collection and recovery services.
  • Follow Kenya's applicable restrictions and regulations on plastic products.

Hazardous and Electronic Waste

Batteries, electronic equipment, pesticides, used oils, chemicals and other hazardous materials require special handling. They should not be burned, buried indiscriminately or disposed of with ordinary household waste.

CLIMATE RESILIENCE

Environmental Conservation and Climate Change

Climate change affects ecosystems, agricultural systems, water resources, biodiversity and community livelihoods. Environmental conservation contributes to climate change adaptation and, in some cases, mitigation.

Climate Change Adaptation

Adaptation involves adjusting natural and human systems to actual or expected climatic conditions. Conservation activities that support adaptation include:

  • Restoring degraded landscapes and natural vegetation.
  • Improving soil moisture retention and water harvesting.
  • Protecting water sources and catchments.
  • Promoting climate-resilient agricultural practices.
  • Improving rangeland management and livestock adaptation.
  • Conserving biodiversity and ecosystem connectivity.

Climate Change Mitigation

Mitigation involves reducing greenhouse gas emissions or increasing the removal of greenhouse gases from the atmosphere. Appropriate actions may include protecting existing forests, restoring degraded ecosystems, improving energy efficiency and adopting suitable low-emission practices.

Carbon benefits should be assessed using credible methods. Tree planting alone does not establish a verified quantity of carbon removed.

PEOPLE AND THE ENVIRONMENT

Community Participation in Environmental Conservation

Environmental conservation is more sustainable when local communities participate in identifying challenges, planning interventions, implementing activities and monitoring results.

Role of Local Communities

  • Identify environmental challenges affecting their local areas.
  • Participate in restoration and tree-protection initiatives.
  • Support sustainable grazing and land-use practices.
  • Protect community water sources.
  • Participate in environmental education and awareness activities.
  • Report pollution and suspected environmental violations through appropriate channels.

Role of Youth

Young people can contribute through environmental education, innovation, community mobilization, restoration activities, research, responsible resource management and the development of practical environmental solutions.

Role of Women

Women contribute important knowledge and leadership in natural resource management, agriculture, household water use, food systems and community development. Inclusive conservation programmes should support meaningful participation and decision-making opportunities for women.

Role of Institutions and Partnerships

Government agencies, schools, universities, community organizations, research institutions, civil society organizations and private-sector partners can support conservation through technical expertise, environmental education, research, funding and implementation.

ACTION AT COMMUNITY LEVEL

Practical Environmental Conservation Activities

Conservation becomes meaningful when environmental knowledge is translated into appropriate activities that communities can implement, maintain and evaluate.

Community Tree Growing and Protection

Establish locally suitable tree-growing activities, protect existing indigenous trees and organize follow-up maintenance to improve survival and long-term establishment.

Rangeland Restoration

Work with pastoral communities to identify degraded grazing areas, support appropriate resting and recovery, and restore native vegetation where needed.

Soil and Water Conservation

Promote suitable erosion-control measures, rainwater harvesting, vegetation protection and responsible water use.

Environmental Clean-Up Activities

Organize community clean-up activities with safe waste collection, sorting and disposal arrangements. Clean-up events should be supported by a plan for preventing waste from accumulating again.

Environmental Education

Conduct community awareness sessions, school activities, field demonstrations and educational campaigns about conservation and sustainable natural resource use.

Biodiversity Observation

Encourage responsible observation and recording of local plants, birds, wildlife and other biodiversity. Avoid disturbing sensitive species or habitats.

Waste Reduction Initiatives

Promote waste separation, responsible recycling, suitable composting and reduced use of unnecessary disposable materials.

FROM PLANNING TO IMPLEMENTATION

How to Plan an Environmental Conservation Project

A successful conservation project should respond to an identified environmental problem, use appropriate technical approaches, involve affected communities and include arrangements for maintenance and monitoring.

  1. Identify the environmental problem. Determine whether the main concern involves land degradation, vegetation loss, waste, water pollution, biodiversity decline or another environmental issue.
  2. Assess the local environment. Study the location, soil, vegetation, water availability, land use, community activities and other relevant environmental conditions.
  3. Consult the community. Engage residents, land users, pastoralists, farmers, local leaders and other relevant stakeholders.
  4. Set clear objectives. Define what the project intends to achieve, the area involved, expected results and the beneficiaries.
  5. Select appropriate interventions. Choose measures based on local ecological conditions and technical advice rather than adopting a one-size-fits-all approach.
  6. Establish responsibilities and resources. Identify personnel, materials, technical support, funding, permissions and maintenance responsibilities.
  7. Implement the activities. Carry out the project with community participation, appropriate safeguards and suitable technical supervision.
  8. Monitor progress. Collect information about activities, environmental conditions and measurable results.
  9. Maintain and improve the project. Address problems, replace failed interventions where appropriate and adjust the management approach.
  10. Report and share lessons. Document achievements, challenges, lessons learned and recommendations for future conservation work.

Useful Environmental Monitoring Indicators

  • Area of land under restoration or improved management.
  • Survival and condition of planted trees after defined monitoring periods.
  • Changes in vegetation cover and ground protection.
  • Condition of selected rangeland or restoration sites.
  • Number of households or community members participating.
  • Quantity and type of waste properly collected or recovered.
  • Water quality or water availability indicators, where monitored.
  • Biodiversity observations using consistent methods.
ENVIRONMENTAL GOVERNANCE IN KENYA

Kenya's Environmental Laws and Institutions

Environmental conservation in Kenya is supported by constitutional provisions, national legislation, policies, regulations and public institutions. These frameworks establish responsibilities for environmental protection, sustainable resource management, public participation and enforcement.

1. Constitution of Kenya, 2010

Article 42 recognizes the right to a clean and healthy environment. Article 69 establishes obligations relating to sustainable management and conservation of the environment and natural resources. Article 70 provides for enforcement of environmental rights.

2. Environmental Management and Co-ordination Act (EMCA), 1999

EMCA provides Kenya's principal framework for environmental management and coordination. It establishes NEMA and provides for environmental planning, environmental impact assessment, environmental audits, monitoring, restoration and conservation measures.

3. Climate Change Act, 2016

This legislation provides a framework for coordinated responses to climate change, including climate governance and the integration of climate considerations into development planning.

4. Forest Conservation and Management Act, 2016

This Act provides a framework for the conservation, protection and sustainable management of forest resources in Kenya.

5. Sustainable Waste Management Act, 2022

This Act establishes a framework for sustainable waste management, including waste reduction, resource recovery and responsibilities across the waste management chain.

6. Wildlife Conservation and Management Act, 2013

This Act provides a framework for wildlife conservation and management, including the protection and sustainable management of wildlife resources.

7. Water Act, 2016

The Water Act provides a framework for water resource management and water services. Water resource activities should comply with applicable authorization and regulatory requirements.

Important Environmental Institutions

  • National Environment Management Authority (NEMA): Coordinates and supervises environmental management and implements environmental policies.
  • Kenya Forest Service (KFS): Supports the conservation and sustainable management of forest resources.
  • Kenya Forestry Research Institute (KEFRI): Undertakes forestry research and provides scientific information relevant to tree growing, forestry and ecosystem management.
  • Kenya Wildlife Service (KWS): Supports wildlife conservation and management.
  • Water Resources Authority (WRA): Regulates and manages water resources within its statutory mandate.
  • Kenya Meteorological Department: Provides meteorological information and services relevant to climate awareness and planning.
  • County Governments: Carry out relevant county environmental, natural resource and development functions under the Constitution and applicable legislation.

Legal guidance: This page is an educational resource, not legal advice. Environmental laws and regulations may be amended. Before undertaking projects involving protected areas, forests, water resources, waste facilities, wildlife or activities requiring environmental assessment, consult the responsible authority and check the current legal requirements.

NATIONAL RESTORATION PRIORITIES

Kenya's Landscape and Ecosystem Restoration Strategy

Kenya's National Landscape and Ecosystem Restoration Strategy 2023–2032 provides a national direction for restoring degraded landscapes and ecosystems. It recognizes that restoration approaches should respond to different ecological zones and land-use systems.

Relevant restoration approaches include forest restoration, agroforestry, restoration of degraded rangelands, soil and water conservation, catchment protection, biodiversity conservation and sustainable land management.

Practical Relevance to Communities

  • Protect existing natural vegetation and forests.
  • Restore degraded rangelands and agricultural landscapes.
  • Promote suitable agroforestry systems.
  • Protect water catchments and riparian areas.
  • Improve soil and water conservation practices.
  • Strengthen community participation in restoration activities.

The strategy is especially relevant when designing conservation activities that link environmental restoration with agricultural development and community livelihoods.

GLOBAL ENVIRONMENTAL COMMITMENTS

United Nations Environmental Conservation Frameworks

Environmental conservation is also guided by international agreements and initiatives that encourage countries, institutions and communities to protect nature, restore ecosystems and promote sustainable development.

1. United Nations Sustainable Development Goals

The 2030 Agenda for Sustainable Development includes goals that directly relate to environmental conservation.

  • SDG 6 – Clean Water and Sanitation: Promotes water availability, water quality and sustainable water resource management.
  • SDG 12 – Responsible Consumption and Production: Encourages efficient resource use, waste reduction and responsible production.
  • SDG 13 – Climate Action: Supports climate adaptation, mitigation and resilience.
  • SDG 14 – Life Below Water: Promotes the conservation and sustainable use of marine resources.
  • SDG 15 – Life on Land: Supports the protection, restoration and sustainable use of terrestrial ecosystems, forests, biodiversity and land resources.

2. United Nations Decade on Ecosystem Restoration 2021–2030

The United Nations Decade on Ecosystem Restoration is a global initiative to prevent, halt and reverse ecosystem degradation. It promotes restoration across forests, grasslands, agricultural lands, wetlands, rivers, coastal areas and other ecosystems.

Its principles support community participation, ecosystem protection, restoration planning and long-term environmental sustainability.

3. Kunming–Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework

Adopted under the Convention on Biological Diversity in 2022, this framework sets global goals and targets for protecting biodiversity, restoring ecosystems and promoting the sustainable use of nature.

Its targets include conserving important areas, restoring degraded ecosystems, reducing threats to biodiversity and improving the participation and benefits of communities.

4. United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change

The UNFCCC provides an international framework for addressing climate change. It supports international cooperation on mitigation, adaptation, climate resilience and climate-related action.

5. Convention on Biological Diversity

The Convention on Biological Diversity promotes biodiversity conservation, sustainable use of biological resources and fair and equitable sharing of benefits arising from genetic resources.
