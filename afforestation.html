Growing a Greener Future

Afforestation is the establishment of forest cover on land that has not been recently forested. It involves planting suitable trees or supporting natural tree regeneration to increase vegetation cover, restore degraded land and improve environmental conditions.

Reforestation, on the other hand, involves restoring tree cover in areas where forests have previously existed but have been removed or severely degraded. Both approaches contribute to landscape restoration, biodiversity conservation and sustainable natural resource management.

Agroforestry is an agricultural land-use system that deliberately integrates trees and shrubs with crops and livestock. It allows farmers to combine agricultural production with tree-based products and environmental services on the same farm or landscape.

Trees can provide fruits, fodder, fuelwood, timber, shade, shelter, medicines and other valuable resources. They can also improve soil protection, support biodiversity and help farming communities adapt to changing climatic conditions.

Goreale Agricultural Innovators promotes responsible tree growing, community participation, environmental education, research and sustainable agroforestry practices.

Types of Agroforestry Systems

Agroforestry can be practised in different ways depending on the farm environment, farmer objectives, available resources and local agricultural activities.

1. Agrisilvicultural Systems

These systems combine trees and agricultural crops on the same land. Trees may be planted along farm boundaries, in rows, around crop fields or within selected crop production areas.

Examples include growing suitable fruit trees with vegetables, planting trees along crop field boundaries and integrating nitrogen-fixing trees into farming systems where appropriate.

2. Silvopastoral Systems

Silvopastoral systems combine trees, shrubs, pasture and livestock. Trees can provide shade, shelter, supplementary fodder and environmental protection in grazing areas.

These systems are particularly relevant to livestock production in arid and semi-arid regions when tree species and grazing pressure are properly managed.

3. Agrosilvopastoral Systems

These systems combine trees, crops and livestock within an integrated land-use system. Farmers may grow crops, maintain suitable fodder trees and keep livestock while managing the available land and water resources.

4. Farm Woodlots and Boundary Planting

Farmers establish selected trees in designated areas or along farm boundaries. These trees may provide poles, fuelwood, shade, fruits and other useful products while helping define farm boundaries.

Suitable Tree Species for Drylands

Selecting the right tree species is one of the most important decisions in afforestation and agroforestry. A species that grows well in one location may perform poorly in another because of differences in rainfall, soil type, temperature, water availability and pests.

In northern Kenya and other dryland areas, priority should be given to locally adapted and appropriate indigenous species, based on advice from forestry and agricultural specialists.

Melia volkensii

Commonly known as Mukau. A valuable dryland tree associated with timber production and farm-based tree growing. It requires suitable site conditions and proper establishment.

Balanites aegyptiaca

A hardy dryland tree with several traditional uses. It can provide fruits, browse and other useful products in suitable environments.

Acacia senegal

A dryland species known for gum arabic production. It can support livelihood diversification and suitable restoration initiatives.

Ziziphus mauritiana

A fruit-producing tree that can contribute to household nutrition and income where soil and climate conditions are suitable.

Salvadora persica

A useful dryland shrub or small tree traditionally valued for several purposes, including local uses and environmental protection.

Cordia sinensis

A dryland tree with potential uses including fodder, fuelwood and other local products, depending on site suitability and management.

Important Tree Selection Advice

These are examples, not a universal planting list. Before planting, confirm species suitability with KEFRI, local forestry officers or qualified agricultural extension personnel. Avoid introducing invasive species, and protect existing native vegetation and sensitive ecosystems.

Tree Nursery Establishment and Management

A tree nursery is a place where seeds, cuttings and other planting materials are propagated and raised into healthy seedlings before they are transferred to their permanent growing locations.

Proper nursery management is essential because healthy, well-developed seedlings have a better opportunity to establish successfully after planting.

Important Nursery Activities

  • Select a suitable nursery location with accessible water.
  • Identify reliable sources of quality seeds and planting materials.
  • Use suitable soil and growing media for each species.
  • Prepare clean containers, seedbeds or propagation materials.
  • Provide appropriate watering without waterlogging seedlings.
  • Control weeds, pests and diseases using suitable methods.
  • Provide shade where necessary during early seedling development.
  • Remove weak or diseased seedlings from the nursery.
  • Gradually harden seedlings before field planting.
  • Maintain records of seed sources, germination and seedling production.

Quality Seedlings Matter

Seedlings should be healthy, properly rooted, free from serious pests and diseases, and suitable for the intended planting environment. Poor-quality planting material can reduce survival and waste time, labour and resources.

Proper Tree Planting Techniques

Tree planting involves more than placing a seedling in the ground. Good site preparation, correct planting procedures, suitable timing and regular maintenance all influence tree establishment and survival.

1. Select the Planting Site

Assess the soil, drainage, sunlight, rainfall, land use and available water. Avoid planting trees where they may damage infrastructure or compete excessively with important crops.

2. Select Suitable Seedlings

Choose healthy seedlings of species adapted to the local environment and the purpose of the planting project.

3. Prepare the Planting Hole

Prepare an appropriately sized hole according to the seedling and soil conditions. Loosen compacted soil where necessary and preserve suitable topsoil.

4. Plant at the Right Time

In rain-fed dryland areas, plant when reliable rainfall has sufficiently wetted the soil. Avoid planting immediately before an expected long dry spell.

5. Position the Seedling Correctly

Remove the container carefully without damaging the roots. Position the seedling upright and keep the root collar at the appropriate soil level.

6. Firm the Soil

Refill the hole and gently firm the soil around the root system to remove large air pockets without excessively compacting it.

7. Conserve Soil Moisture

Use suitable organic mulch around the seedling, leaving a small gap around the stem. Consider water-harvesting structures where appropriate.

8. Protect and Monitor the Tree

Protect young trees from livestock, wildlife, termites and other threats. Check survival regularly and replace dead seedlings when conditions are suitable.

Afforestation in Arid and Semi-Arid Areas

Dryland environments experience low or unreliable rainfall, high temperatures, water shortages and periods of drought. These conditions make tree establishment challenging and require careful planning.

In Garissa and other parts of northern Kenya, afforestation programmes should consider local ecological conditions, livestock production, community needs and competition for limited water.

Dryland Tree-Growing Strategies

  • Prioritize drought-tolerant and locally adapted tree species.
  • Plant when soil moisture is adequate.
  • Use planting basins and micro-catchments to collect runoff where suitable.
  • Apply organic mulch to reduce moisture loss.
  • Protect young trees from livestock browsing.
  • Use appropriate spacing based on species and water availability.
  • Encourage natural regeneration where suitable mature trees remain.
  • Use community participation to protect restored areas.
  • Monitor seedling survival during dry seasons.

Water Conservation Is Essential

Water is one of the most important factors affecting tree survival in drylands. Rainwater harvesting, soil moisture conservation, suitable planting basins and careful seedling management can improve establishment. Any supplementary watering plan should consider the availability and sustainable use of local water resources.

Environmental Benefits

Well-planned afforestation, forest restoration and agroforestry initiatives can improve environmental conditions and contribute to healthier landscapes.

  • Soil protection: Tree roots help hold soil together and reduce erosion.
  • Water conservation: Vegetation can slow surface runoff and support water infiltration.
  • Climate regulation: Trees store carbon as they grow and can provide shade.
  • Biodiversity: Trees provide habitats and resources for birds, insects and other wildlife.
  • Wind protection: Suitable shelterbelts can reduce wind speed and protect crops.
  • Soil improvement: Leaf litter and organic matter can contribute to soil health.
  • Land restoration: Appropriate vegetation can help restore degraded landscapes.
  • Desertification control: Integrated land management can help reduce land degradation.

Tree planting must nevertheless be planned carefully. Poorly selected species, excessive planting density and inappropriate land conversion can place additional pressure on water resources and natural ecosystems.

Tree Maintenance and Protection

Successful afforestation depends on what happens after planting. Newly established trees need protection and appropriate care until they become sufficiently established.

Essential Maintenance Practices

  • Inspect young trees regularly for signs of stress.
  • Provide supplementary water when necessary and sustainable.
  • Remove competing weeds around young seedlings.
  • Maintain suitable mulch without covering the tree stem.
  • Repair protective fences and tree guards.
  • Monitor termites, pests and diseases.
  • Replace dead seedlings when suitable planting conditions return.
  • Prune only when necessary and according to species requirements.
  • Prevent uncontrolled grazing in restoration areas.
  • Record tree growth and survival rates.

Long-Term Tree Management

Tree-growing projects should have a maintenance plan and clear responsibility for protecting planted trees. Monitoring should continue beyond the planting day to assess survival, growth, environmental outcomes and community benefits.

Research, Technology and Innovation

Research and innovation can improve the planning, implementation and monitoring of afforestation and agroforestry programmes. Evidence-based decisions help identify appropriate species, suitable planting methods and effective land management practices.

Areas for Research and Innovation

  • Testing the performance of locally adapted tree species.
  • Researching seed germination and nursery production methods.
  • Evaluating water-harvesting techniques for tree establishment.
  • Studying interactions between trees, crops and livestock.
  • Using digital mapping to monitor tree cover and restoration areas.
  • Measuring seedling survival and long-term tree growth.
  • Exploring markets for sustainable tree-based products.

Technology such as GPS mapping, satellite imagery, mobile data collection and digital monitoring tools can support project planning and evaluation.
